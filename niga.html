Part 1

Religious

Earth as Relationship, not just Resource

"The Earth is mother; I am son of Earth."

— Atharva Veda 12.1.12

In this ancient text, Earth is represented through kinship. Humans are not imagined as outside the Earth; they belong to her. This relational view directly contrasts with a purely instrumental view where Earth is merely a storehouse of resources.

Isha Upanishad (1, 6–7)

This text pairs enjoyment with renunciation rather than unlimited possession. It describes a profound vision where the wise person sees all beings within the Self and the Self in all beings, challenging a rigid human–nature separation.

Ramayana (Aranya Kanda)

The forest is represented as a place of immense beauty and a hub of spiritual life. However, it is also a space that humans travel through, inhabit, and use. Nature here is revered, yet intertwined with human purposes, complicating the divide between nature and culture.

Part 2

Urbanisation

The Wasteland as Lived Experience

What happens when human desires become embedded in industrial, urban, and climate-scale systems?

■ Aaranyak (Bibhutibhushan Bandyopadhyay)

Represents the tragedy of profound environmental change. The text captures the sorrow of pristine forests being systematically destroyed to make way for agricultural settlement and the early stages of human urbanization.

■ Bengaluru Lakes (E.g., Yele Mallappa Shetty)

Modern journalism reveals lakes facing untreated sewage, massive encroachment, and construction debris. Shrinking water bodies and drains lead to reduced groundwater recharge and drastically increased flood risk—a consequence of commodification and anthropocentrism converging.

The Hungry Tide

Amitav Ghosh portrays the Sundarbans as a space where boundaries between land and water blur. Here, nature is not scenery; it is an active force shaping human survival.

Kadvi Hawa (Film)

The "wasteland" is shown not just as a visually damaged landscape, but as a social condition. Climate change creates profound livelihood insecurity, debt, and survival crises.

Part 3

Spiritual

The Tree as an Ethical Mirror & Sacred Rivers under Pressure

A Sacred River Under Pressure

Rivers like the Ganga possess deep sacred and cultural significance. Yet, large ritual gatherings and practices create massive material pressure on the riverbanks. During idol immersion, non-eco-friendly materials introduce plaster, synthetic paints, and plastics into the water body.

The Ecocritical Gap: There is a stark contradiction between symbolic value and ecological value. A sacred river can be deeply respected spiritually, while simultaneously being treated materially as a place that can absorb limitless waste.

Kabir: The Ethical Mirror

In the medieval period, nature increasingly works as a metaphor and ethical mirror to question human desire, status, and excess.

"Bada hua to kya hua, jaise ped khajur…"

Compares superficial greatness with a date-palm that gives little shade and whose fruit is difficult to reach. The tree becomes a measure of usefulness and humility, critiquing human excess.

"Sai itna dijiye…"

A plea asking only for enough to sustain a household and a guest—an ethic of sufficiency rather than limitless accumulation and commodification.

Varanasi riverfront: inseparable from living traditions, yet facing modern pressures.

Conclusion

The alternative is not to abandon culture, cities, or technology. It is to make them compatible with ecological limits. Humans can live with nature when it is treated not merely as a resource outside us, but as the living system of which we are a part.
